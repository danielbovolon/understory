# How it lives

Understory is built from what is known about mycorrhizal fungi and forests. Where the science is disputed, the system lets the behaviour emerge instead of hard-coding it. Every figure that is scaled for listening is marked as scaled.

## Two clocks
- **The sky runs on the real clock.** A forest opens at your real local time, on today's date, under a sun computed for an Alpine valley (46.5° N).
  - This covers sunrise and sunset, day length through the year, daily and seasonal temperature, rain, wind, and the night.
  - At 1× a forest day lasts a day. The Time control runs the clock from 0.25× to 24×.
  - The Season control swaps today's date for May, July, October or January.
- **Life is compressed.** Real forests take decades and real hyphae grow millimetres a day. Here growth is compressed, and every tree species has a lifespan: pioneers such as birch and alder live shortest, stone pine and oak longest. Old trees lose vigour, die as snags and open gaps for the next generation, so a forest left running keeps shifting, sometimes into a different forest altogether. Seeds keep arriving on the wind and with jays and nutcrackers, and they take where light reaches the floor. Trees carry reserves through the night (dark respiration is lower), and shade-tolerant species such as fir and beech get by on little light, while pioneers starve under a closed canopy.
- **Reserves bridge the gap.** Trees store sugar, so a forest started at night still grows. It grows more slowly until the sun comes up.
- **Sound always plays in real time.**

## Succession
Every forest starts as **bare soil** and grows through seven stages. Each new stage is announced in the log.

| Stage | Typically at 1× | What has happened |
|---|---|---|
| Bare soil | 0 min | Seeds lie on the centres of the flower of life. Only wind and the soil are audible. |
| Germination | ~0.5 min | Seedlings break the surface one by one. They live on their seed reserves and sing an octave higher, with a single partial. |
| First partners | ~1–6 min | Fungal spores blown in on the wind germinate near young roots and form the first mycorrhizal mantles. |
| Network | ~5–15 min | Several trees share fungal partners. Sugar, nutrients, water and signals start to move. |
| Young forest | ~25 min | Trees put on height and gain partials. Fungi have stored enough of their hosts' sugar to fruit. |
| Closing canopy | ~40 min | Shade spreads and litter builds up. Late-stage fungi arrive, then decomposers. |
| Old growth | ~60 min | Hub trees, thick cords, seedlings raised in the shade. |

**Early-stage and late-stage fungi.** Studies of ectomycorrhizal succession found pioneer genera arriving first on young hosts and other genera joining as stands age. On the Lyman Glacier forefront, for example, *Cortinarius*, *Inocybe* and *Laccaria* came first, and *Lactarius* and *Suillus* appeared only on older ground.
- Each seed draws two early-stage genera, two late-stage genera and one decomposer.
- Spores of early-stage fungi arrive from the start. Late-stage spores come once there is a canopy, and decomposers once there is litter.
- Trees reward partners that suit their age: early-stage fungi earn most from young trees, late-stage fungi from old ones. So the fungal community turns over as the forest matures.

**Spores need roots.** The germination of ectomycorrhizal spores is stimulated by host roots. In Understory a drifting spore only germinates within reach of a root, or on litter for decomposers.

## Exploration types
Agerer classified ectomycorrhizal mycelium by how far it reaches out from the root tip. Each species here has one of these body plans:

| Type | Body plan in the system | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| **Contact** | Slow, densely branching, short-lived, cheap. | Fine high ticks. |
| **Short-distance** | Like contact, a little further reaching. | Ticks. |
| **Medium-distance** | Moderate speed, fusing networks, moderate cords. | Mid-register signals. |
| **Long-distance** | Fast, straight and rarely branching, with long-lived thick cords. Carries more organic nitrogen and water. Faster signals. Expensive. | Low, longer chirps; deep tick filter. |

## Hyphal growth
- Each tip takes a persistent random walk. Ectomycorrhizal tips are steered toward unreached roots and toward nutrient patches. Decomposer tips are steered toward litter.
- Tips branch behind the apex and die of age or at the plot edge.
- **Anastomosis:** tips of the same individual fuse into loops.
- **Incompatibility:** tips of different individuals wall themselves off. You hear a short crackle.

## The market
Kiers et al. (2011) showed that plants and mycorrhizal fungi reward each other in proportion to what the partner supplies: more carbon to fungi that deliver more phosphorus, and vice versa.
- **Foraging:** fungi take nutrients from mineral and organic patches in the soil, plus a thin background trickle along all their hyphae. Long-distance types are better at organic nitrogen.
- **Delivery:** each fungus hands nutrients to the trees it is bonded to. Some genomes are greedy and hold part of it back.
- **Payment:** every tree passes a share of its fresh photosynthate below ground, split among its partners by what each has delivered recently.
  - Nutrient-starved trees send more, up to about 58 %. Saturated trees send less, down to about 18 %. Real forests send roughly 10–30 % of their production to their fungi.
  - The share also leans toward partners suited to the tree's age.
- **Sound:** a delivery sounds as a soft, high tone at the root.

## Fruiting
- **Fruit comes from the trees' sugar.** Fruit bodies are built from the host's recent photosynthate: cutting the bark ring (girdling) stopped ectomycorrhizal fruiting within days (Högberg et al. 2001). In Understory a fungus saves part of its carbon income for fruiting and fruits only when that store is full.
- **Triggers:**
  - wet soil
  - cool weather
  - the hours after rain
  - the season, with autumn strongest
- **Fairy rings:** fruit bodies form at the advancing margin of the colony, which is how fairy rings arise.
- **Spore launch:** each spore is flicked off the gill by Buller's drop, a tiny surface-tension catapult. You hear it as a short, very high click.
- **Lift:** evaporation under the cap cools the air and drives a small convective flow that carries spores upward even in still air (Dressaire et al. 2016). Spores then drift on the wind with turbulence and settle slowly.
- **Offspring:** a spore that lands near a root, or on litter for decomposers, can germinate into a new individual. Its genome is mutated: growth, branching, timbre, register, resonator, fruit voice, trade behaviour and colour.

## Decomposers and foxfire
- **Litter** comes from leaf and needle fall (more in autumn), dead fruit bodies, pruned hyphae and dead trees.
- **Decomposers** (*Mycena*, *Panellus*, *Armillaria*, *Marasmius*, *Gymnopus*) grow toward litter, break it down and release nutrient patches that the mycorrhizal fungi then forage. That closes the nutrient cycle.
- **Glow:** some decomposers glow. In *Neonothopanus gardneri* the glow is controlled by a circadian clock, and the light attracts insects that may carry spores (Oliveira et al. 2015). Here luminous species glow only in darkness, and a faint shimmer hangs over them at night.

## Water by night
Querejeta et al. (2003) tracked dye released by the taproots of oak seedlings at night into their ectomycorrhizal and arbuscular mycorrhizal hyphae. The water came from hydraulic lift, and the transfer happened only at night and only through intact hyphal connections. Rhizomorph-forming *Cortinarius* kept distant hyphae supplied longest.
- **In the system:** at night, deep-rooted mature trees send water through the hyphal network toward dry seedlings, along the actual path of the hyphae.
- **Sound:** a soft liquid gurgle moves through space. A blue bead shows it in the view.

## Stress sounds
Khait et al. (2023) recorded airborne ultrasonic clicks (20–100 kHz) from tomato and tobacco plants: about one an hour when well watered, up to about 35 an hour when drought-stressed or cut. Air bubbles in the xylem are the leading explanation.
- Here every tree clicks at those rates, scaled by tree size.
- Drought stress comes from soil moisture, sunlight and heat.
- The clicks are transposed down about tenfold into hearing range.
- A dashed amber ring marks a thirsty tree.

## Electrical signalling
- **Spike trains.** Adamatzky recorded trains of electrical spikes in fungal mycelia, grouped them into "words", and estimated a lexicon of up to about 50 words, with most species using 15–20. Whether this is language is open.
- **After rain.** Fukasawa et al. (2023) measured the ectomycorrhizal *Laccaria bicolor* in the field. Electrical potentials rose and fluctuated after rainfall, sometimes above 100 mV. Signals appeared to pass between nearby fruit bodies in a particular direction, and the activity correlated with precipitation and temperature.
- **In the system:**
  - Each node integrates its input and fires a train when it crosses a threshold, then rests.
  - Spontaneous firing rises sharply after rain and with warmth.
  - Nodes fire when a tip discovers a nutrient patch and when a fruit body matures.
- **Speed is scaled.** Real propagation is far slower; here signals travel at walking pace so you hear them pass.

## Alarms
- Damaged plants are reported to signal through shared fungal networks, and neighbours prepare their defences.
- A browsed tree, from deer every 10–20 minutes, or a tree you wound sends faster, longer trains that travel further.
- Trees that hear them shift toward inharmonic partials, brighten and tremble, then relax over about a minute.

## Carbon between trees
- **What was measured.** Simard et al. (1997) measured carbon moving between birch and Douglas fir through shared fungi. Klein et al. (2016) estimated that about 4 % of fine-root carbon was exchanged between neighbouring trees in a Swiss forest.
- **What is disputed.** A 2023 review (Karst, Jones & Hoeksema) found little peer-reviewed evidence that "mother trees" preferentially feed their kin, and found the evidence that transfer helps seedlings to be thin.
- **So in Understory:** sugar moves only by pressure differences.
  - Shaded seedlings are sinks and sunlit trees are sources.
  - Hub trees are simply the most connected at the moment.
  - Nothing preferential is coded.

## Transport and adaptation
- **Flow:** tree sugar sets the pressure at tree nodes. Junction pressures relax to the conductance-weighted mean of their neighbours, and flow on each hypha is its conductance divided by its length, times the pressure difference. This is the Physarum transport model (Tero et al. 2010).
- **Thickening:** conductance grows with flow and decays without it. Cords that lead to a discovered nutrient patch also thicken, and cords of a trading fungus are maintained. This follows observations of cord-forming fungi (Bebber, Fricker, Boddy).
- **Pruning:** hyphae that thin out below a threshold are pruned and become litter.

## Places
Every spot in the forest has its own conditions, held in a soil map of about 1.6 m cells.
- **Site wetness.** Each seed lays a wet seep and a dry ridge across the ground. Daily soil moisture is the weather plus the local wetness.
- **Fertility, acidity, stone.** These vary in patches; the ridge is stony and poor.
- **Light.** It falls through the canopy: larger trees in leaf shade the cells beneath them.
- **Reshaping.** The Shape ground tool rewrites these conditions. Painted ground keeps its new character and drifts back only very slowly.
- **Trees change the ground:**
  - Conifer needles acidify the soil beneath them.
  - Alder fixes nitrogen through its root symbiosis with *Frankia* and enriches the soil.
  - Beech and oak leaf litter enriches it slightly.
  - Rotting logs build rich soil and feed decomposers.

## Tree species
Eight species of the Alpine valley and montane forests are modelled. Each has a niche: preferred wetness, acidity and fertility, plus tolerance of shade and stony ground.

| Species | Niche | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| European larch | light-demanding pioneer, stony ground | turns gold and drops its needles; *Suillus* partner |
| Swiss stone pine | poor, acid, stony, high | evergreen; seeds cached by nutcrackers; *Suillus*, *Rhizopogon* |
| Norway spruce | moist, acid, shade-tolerant | evergreen; acidifies the soil strongly |
| Silver fir | moist, fertile, very shade-tolerant | evergreen |
| European beech | fertile, shade-tolerant | keeps dry brown leaves through winter (marcescence) |
| Silver birch | pioneer of open ground, tolerant | bright gold in autumn, bare in winter; *Leccinum* partner |
| Black alder | wet ground, streams and bogs | fixes nitrogen; drops green leaves; *Alnicola* partner |
| Sessile oak | drier, moderately fertile | marcescent; acorns cached by jays |

- **Fit to the place** controls germination and establishment, growth, and which species the place chooses when you plant with "Place decides".
- **Leaves:** deciduous trees leaf out from late April to mid May, colour in October and stand bare from November. Bare trees barely photosynthesise and have quieter, breathier voices.

## Fungal ecology
- **Host specificity.** Most ectomycorrhizal fungi partner many trees, but some are specialists: *Suillus* with larch and pines, *Rhizopogon* with pines, *Leccinum* with birch, *Alnicola* with alder (Molina et al. 1992). Specialists only bond with, and only germinate near, their hosts.
- **Soil preferences.** Each genus has a preferred acidity and wetness.
  - Many ectomycorrhizal fungi, notably *Cortinarius*, *Russula* and *Tricholoma*, decline on nitrogen-rich soil (Lilleskov et al. 2002); here they grow and trade less on rich ground.
  - *Cenococcum geophilum* is drought-tolerant and forms no fruit bodies.
- **Fruiting calendars.** Every genus fruits in its own months:
  - *Hygrophorus marzuolus* in March and April, at the snowmelt
  - most genera from summer to autumn
  - *Flammulina velutipes* (velvet shank) in winter, even in frost
- **Spore rain follows the calendar,** so the species arriving on the wind change through the year.

## Ground cover
The herb and moss layer is computed for every cell from light, wetness, acidity, stone and fertility:

| Cover | Where | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| grass | open, light ground | rustles in the wind; dense grass smothers tree seedlings |
| moss | moist shade | keeps the ground moist; good spore seedbed; softens footsteps and the soil's crackle |
| ferns | moist, fertile half-shade | shade out seedlings; rust-coloured in autumn |
| blueberry heath | acid ground with some light | ericoid mycorrhizal shrubs; hinder tree seedlings; red in autumn |
| sedge | boggy ground | wet footsteps |
| lichen on stone | stony ground | stone footsteps |
| leaf litter | shaded, drier ground | crisp footsteps in autumn |

Spring flowers appear on fertile, light ground. Snow covers everything but stone.

## Walking
The listener can walk on its own at three speeds: about 0.6 m/s ambling, 1.35 m/s walking and 3.3 m/s running. In Rhythm mode it ambles, walks, runs now and then, and walks again. The path meanders, stays inside the circle and steps around trunks. Walking is silent: only the forest is heard.

## Scale
A forest can hold 64 trees and 128 fungal individuals, with networks of up to about three thousand hyphae. On its own the forest fills about 30 trees, 30 mycorrhizal fungi and 12 decomposers; the rest is room for what you plant and sow.
- **Voices:** only the 12 most audible trees (by size, leaf and distance from you) have a voice at once. As you move, voices cross-fade.
- **Foreground:** the foreground cap keeps the forest's own events sparse however large the network grows.

## Happenings
- **Windstorm.** Wind loads trees until branches break and weakened trees are thrown.
  - A thrown tree's root plate tears up mineral soil, and the gap in the canopy lets light reach the floor.
  - Gap regeneration is how many forests renew themselves.
  - Here the storm adds litter, lifts spores from fruit bodies, may throw a stressed or old tree, exposes a mineral nutrient patch, and schedules a seedling in the gap.
- **Dry spell.**
  - Soil dries, so trees click more under drought (Khait et al. 2023).
  - Deep-rooted trees pass water to seedlings through the hyphae at night (Querejeta et al. 2003).
  - Fungi whose rhizomorphs carry water keep trading longest. The study found this for *Cortinarius*; here, long-distance exploration types get the advantage.
- **Seed-caching birds.**
  - The spotted nutcracker (*Nucifraga caryocatactes*) buries Swiss stone pine seeds in thousands of small caches. The caches it never recovers are the main way *Pinus cembra* regenerates.
  - Eurasian jays do the same with acorns and spread oak woods.
  - Forests named after conifers get nutcrackers; the others get jays.
  - In the system a third of the caches are forgotten and sprout a few minutes later.
- **Squirrels and mycophagy.**
  - Squirrels and other small mammals eat mushrooms and dig up truffles (hypogeous fungi).
  - The spores survive the gut and are deposited elsewhere in the droppings. Many truffle-forming ectomycorrhizal fungi, such as *Rhizopogon*, rely on this.
  - The squirrel eats a fruit body if one is standing, digs near trees, disturbs a few hyphae, and leaves spores that may germinate a minute or two later.

## Wild boar, bark beetles and fog
- **Wild boar** root for truffles, bulbs and grubs. They mix litter into the mineral soil, which speeds decomposition and gives tree seeds bare ground to establish on, and they spread truffle spores in their dung. Here a boar visits a few trees, turns the litter into nutrient patches, tears some hyphae, improves germination around it and plants truffle spores.
- **Bark beetles** (*Ips typographus*) breed under the bark of weakened spruce. They carry blue-stain fungi that help them overcome the tree's defences; a healthy tree drowns them in resin. Here they choose the weakest grown conifer, spruce first. A thirsty or old tree usually dies and stands as a snag; a vigorous one survives. Many bark beetles stridulate, and the larvae chew audibly.
- **Fog** comes with still air. Crowns comb droplets from passing cloud and they drip to the ground (fog drip), and in still, cool air near the ground distant sounds carry further. Here fog dims the light, stills the wind, moistens the soil under the trees and lowers distance loss and air absorption in the engine.

## Ecosystem controls
- **Warmth** shifts the temperature: growth, spiking, rain or snow, frost and the cricket chirp rate.
- **Nitrogen** is deposition from polluted air. Trees take up more on their own and send less carbon to their fungi; many ectomycorrhizal fungi, webcaps and knights first, decline on enriched ground (Lilleskov et al. 2002).
- **Seed rain** and **Spore rain** set how much arrives from outside the plot.
- **Grazing** sets how many deer there are. They browse grown trees (an alarm runs through the network) and bite off seedlings, silver fir and oak first and spruce last (Ammer 1996).

## Every voice its own
- **The seed** blends two sound worlds (struck wood, glass and ice, taut strands, breath and reed, bronze and bells, clay and earth). They steer which generators, partials, decays and colours its species and individuals get.
- **Species** are voiced after real wood acoustics. Spruce and fir are light, stiff and lightly damped (the soundboard woods) and sustain bright partials; dense hardwoods damp more and sound darker; birch, a drum-shell wood, is percussive; stone pine breathes. Each crown sounds in the wind: needles whistle at about 0.2 × wind speed ÷ needle width (an Aeolian tone), broad leaves flutter and rustle, and dry leaves held through winter rattle.
- **Individuals** carry a genotype: small offsets in inharmonicity, brightness, detuning, vibrato, breath and pick position. Seedlings inherit their parent's, so kin sound alike. Every fungal individual is a mutated copy of its parent species.
- **State.** A tree's voice follows its age (seedlings sing an octave up, old giants drop an octave and grow more inharmonic), its ground (wet wood damps, stone hardens), its nutrients (fuller upper partials), its partners (a wider chorus) and its stress. A fungus's ring follows its energy, nutrients and age.
- **Crickets** keep time with the temperature: about seven chirps a minute more for every degree (Dolbear 1897).

## Gathering, seed and spores
- **Seed seasons.** Every species ripens its seed in its own months and only from a certain size: spruce cones from October to March, larch in September and October, beech nuts and acorns in September and October, birch from July to November, alder through the winter, pine cones from November to April, rowan berries and yew arils in late summer, willow and aspen cotton in April and May.
- **Viability.** Orthodox seed (spruce, larch, pine, alder) keeps for years; recalcitrant seed (acorns, beech nuts, maple keys) dies when it dries; willow and aspen seed lives only days. In the pouch, viability falls accordingly; old seed may not come up.
- **Dormancy.** Planted seed lies in the soil before it germinates, longest for stone pine and yew.
- **Spore prints.** A ripe mushroom gives a spore print once; truffles are dug, brackets scraped, and *Cenococcum*, which never fruits, gives its sclerotia. Each packet carries the genotype, and so the voice, of the individual it came from.
- **Mast years.** Beech, oak, spruce, fir and stone pine fruit heavily together every few years and little in between. Seed predators cannot eat a whole mast crop, so more seeds survive (predator satiation). Here a mast year triples the crop, brings more seedlings, and draws jays, nutcrackers, squirrels and boar.

## Newcomers
Seed and spores arrive from far away all the time, but a species establishes only where it finds a niche. As the forest grows richer, niches open, and species no forest starts with arrive:
- **Trees.** Goat willow on wet, open ground; aspen after storms open the canopy; Scots pine on dry, stony, poor ground; rowan and yew, planted by thrushes once songbirds live here (yew only in old, deep shade); sycamore maple on rich, moist soil.
- **Fungi.** Chanterelle and hedgehog mushroom in old forest on clean soil; the brown roll-rim with birch where there is disturbance or nitrogen; *Gomphidius*, which lives on the mycelium of *Suillus* (Olsson et al. 2000) and can only come where *Suillus* is established; truffles, carried in by boar to oak or beech on chalky ground; morels on freshly disturbed ground; tinder fungus and turkey tail on dead broadleaf wood, the red-belted conk on dead conifers, sulphur tuft on logs and stumps.
- The richer and older the forest, the more often newcomers come. When the soil is full, a newcomer takes the place of the weakest colony of its kind.

## Local forms and varieties
- **Fungi.** Every offspring and every somatic mutation moves a lineage a little further from its ancestors. When it has drifted far enough, it becomes a local form: a new name (after its colour, its voice, its ground or its place), a new colour, a shifted niche adapted to where it grows, and a new voice. Many fungal "species" are in fact several cryptic species, told apart only by DNA; in real forests the drift takes many generations.
- **Trees.** Seedlings inherit their parent's genotype. A lineage that has passed through enough generations and drifted far enough becomes a local variety (an ecotype, or provenance in forestry): its niche moves toward the ground it grew on, and its seedlings carry it on.

## Life while you are away
When you return to a forest, it catches up on the time you were gone, up to 30 days of forest life, at a coarser step. Growth, ageing and death, seed and spore rain, new partnerships between fungi and trees, fruiting and the drift of voices go on; storms, beetles, boar and seed caching happen in their seasons. Passing sounds (signals, spores in the air, animals) are not simulated. The sky ends at the real hour, and a short story tells you what changed.

## Birds and animals
- **Songbirds** need forest structure. Here they appear once the canopy has grown and sing in the treetops.
  - The dawn chorus peaks in the hour or two after sunrise, with a smaller chorus at dusk.
  - It is strongest in spring and quietest in winter, and is subdued in rain and storms.
  - Each seed has its own three songbirds, each with a distinct phrase shape.
- **Owls** call at night, most in autumn and winter, when tawny owls establish territories.
- **Woodpeckers** drum on standing dead wood, most in late winter and spring.
  - Dead trees now stand as snags for a while before they crumble into litter.
  - That standing dead wood is habitat for beetles, birds and decomposers.
- **Crickets** sing on warm summer nights, even over open ground. Their chirp rate follows the temperature (Dolbear 1897).
- **Red deer** roar at dusk and night during the autumn rut (September–October).
- **Animal sounds are synthesised, not recorded,** and kept sparse. They have their own small share of the foreground.

## Seasons
- **Winter:**
  - Below about 1.5 °C precipitation falls as snow.
  - The snow cover builds up, melts in warmth and feeds the soil moisture.
  - Fresh snow is highly sound-absorbent, so the cover dulls high frequencies, shortens and dries the reverb, and quietens the soil.
  - In hard frost (below −4 °C at night) large trees can split with a loud frost crack, because the outer wood contracts faster than the core.
- **Spring:** a flush of root and hyphal growth and the strongest dawn chorus.
- **Summer:** crickets and fireflies on warm nights, distant thunder in summer rain, more dry spells.
- **Autumn:** leaf and needle fall feeds the litter, mushrooms fruit most, nutcrackers and jays cache seeds, and red deer are in the rut.

## Where you listen
- **Under the soil.** Sound from above ground reaches you through the earth: strongly low-passed, quieter, with less reverb. The mycelium's own sounds are close.
- **Into the canopy.** You listen 11 m up, where birds and wind are near and the soil is far below.
- **Ride along.** You travel with packets of sugar or water, spike trains, spores or animals as they move through the forest.

## The slow drift of the sound world
This part is aesthetic, not biology. It keeps long listening from settling into a loop. The Change control sets how restless it is.
- **Tonal centre:** every 12–25 minutes it leans toward a related key (a fourth, a fifth, a third or a step), and all voices glide there over minutes.
- **Timbre tide:** a slow random walk brightens and darkens the trees and breathes the reverb.
- **Somatic drift:** every few minutes each fungus's voice mutates a little.
- **Nudges:** your nudges in the Layers panel are biases on top of this, and the forest keeps choosing within them.

## Keeping the space uncrowded
- The forest's own foreground events (signal trains, node rings, trades, clicks, effects) share ten places at once. Animals get a little room of their own.
- When the forest is busy, spontaneous spiking and animal calls thin out instead of piling up.
