How it lives

Places. Every spot has its own wetness, fertility, acidity, stone and light. Trees and fungi grow where the place suits them, moss, grass, ferns and heath follow, and the trees change the soil in turn: needles acidify it, alder enriches it. Shape the ground to build your own forest.

Succession. Every forest starts as bare soil. Seedlings germinate on the flower of life; early-stage fungi (Laccaria, Inocybe, Hebeloma…) arrive first on the wind, late-stage fungi (Russula, Suillus, Amanita…) once there is a canopy, decomposers once there is litter.

Exploration types. Each fungus has a body plan: contact and short-distance types make dense fine mats, long-distance types send out straight, thick cords that carry water and signals faster.

The market. Fungi forage nutrients from patches in the soil and trade them to their trees. Trees pay carbon in proportion to what each partner delivers, and send more below ground when they are short of nutrients.

Flow. Sugar moves between trees by pressure through shared cords; cords that carry it thicken, unused ones wither. At night, deep-rooted trees lift water and pass it through the hyphae to seedlings.

Signals. Spike trains travel along hyphae; activity rises after rain and in warmth. Wounded or browsed trees send alarms. Thirsty trees click.

Animals. Songbirds need a canopy and sing most at dawn; owls call at night; woodpeckers drum on dead trunks. Nutcrackers and jays bury seeds and forget some; squirrels eat fungi and plant their spores elsewhere.

Seasons. Winter snow absorbs sound and hard frost cracks trunks; spring brings the dawn chorus and a flush of growth; summer nights have crickets, fireflies and distant thunder; autumn has falling leaves, mushrooms and the red deer rut.

Voices. Every seed blends two sound worlds; every species is voiced after its wood and crown; every tree carries a genotype its seedlings inherit, and its voice follows its age, ground, nutrients and partners. Every fungus is a mutated individual.

Gathering. Seed ripens on mature trees in its season; mushrooms give spore prints. You plant and sow from your pouch, and seed loses viability as real seed does.

Newcomers. As the forest grows richer, species no forest starts with arrive when their niche opens, and lineages that drift far enough become local forms of their own.

While you are away. The forest lives on. When you come back it catches up on the time you were gone and tells you what changed.

Fruit. Mushrooms are built from the trees' recent sugar. They appear at the colony edge after rain, flick their spores off with tiny clicks, and some decomposers glow at night.
